## Why "would you write me a testimonial?" fails

The default ask puts all the work on the client. "Would you mind writing a testimonial?" hands them a blank page, an unclear deadline, and no idea what you want them to say — so it sits in their inbox until it's forgotten. Testimonials don't fail because clients are ungrateful; they fail because the request creates friction. Remove the friction and the yes rate climbs dramatically.

## Timing beats persuasion

The single biggest factor is *when* you ask. The best moment is the peak-enthusiasm window — right after a win: a successful launch, a result that beat expectations, or an unprompted "this is exactly what we wanted" email. That last one is gold; a client who just praised you is primed to put it in writing. The worst time is weeks after the relationship has cooled and the specifics have faded. Build the ask into your project closeout so it lands while the outcome is fresh — the [Offboarding Checklist](/templates/offboarding-checklist) includes it as a standard step so it never slips.

## Make it a two-minute yes

Lower the effort in three ways. First, ask a couple of specific questions instead of requesting an essay. Second, offer to draft it *for* them based on their answers, for their approval. Third, tell them exactly where it'll appear and how long it should be. Specific and easy always beats open-ended and polite.

## Scripts you can copy

**The direct ask (after a clear win):**

> "Really glad this landed the way it did. Would you be open to a short testimonial I could use on my site? To make it easy, I can draft something from a couple of your answers and send it over for your approval — you'd just tweak or sign off. Two minutes, tops."

**The question-based ask (gets specific quotes):**

> "Rather than put you on the spot for a full write-up, could you answer three quick questions? (1) What problem were you trying to solve before we worked together? (2) What changed after? (3) What would you tell someone considering hiring me? I'll shape your answers into a short quote for your approval."

That three-question structure is the workhorse — it pulls a before, an after, and a recommendation, which is the anatomy of a testimonial that actually converts readers instead of just saying "great to work with."

**The draft-for-approval follow-up:**

> "Here's a short version based on what you shared — feel free to edit anything so it sounds like you. If it's good as-is, just reply 'approved' and I'll take it from there."

## Ask for the referral in the same breath

A happy client is your warmest source of the next one, and the moment they've agreed to a testimonial is the ideal time to ask: "Is there anyone else you know who's wrestling with the same problem?" You're not being pushy — you're catching them at peak goodwill. ## Make it usable

When you get the approved quote, ask two more small things: permission to use their name, role, and company (an anonymous testimonial is far weaker), and — if it fits — a headshot or logo. A named, specific, credentialed quote does the persuasive work; a vague anonymous one just fills space.
