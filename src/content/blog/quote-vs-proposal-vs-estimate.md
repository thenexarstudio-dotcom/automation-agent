## Why this mix-up costs you deals

A prospect emails asking "how much would this cost?" and a lot of freelancers respond with a five-page proposal, complete with a bio, case studies, and a process diagram. The prospect wanted a number to see if you're in their range. Instead they got homework — so they don't reply. The reverse mistake is just as common: someone asks for a full proposal on a complex engagement, and gets back a one-line price with no context, no scope, and nothing to justify it. Neither mismatch is a pricing problem. It's a document problem. Quotes, estimates, and proposals answer different questions, and sending the right one at the right stage is one of the highest-leverage things you can fix in your sales process.

## Quote: a fast, itemized price to qualify budget

A quote is a firm, itemized price for defined work — usually one to three line items, sent within a day of the request. Its job isn't to sell; it's to qualify. Someone asks "how much for a logo and a one-page site," you send a quote with a number attached, and within minutes you both know whether this conversation is worth continuing. A quote should be specific enough to hold you to it (list what's included, what's not, and how long it's valid) but light enough that writing one takes ten minutes, not two hours. If the request is simple and well-defined — a known deliverable, a known scope — a quote is the right and only document you need. ## Estimate: a rough ballpark, deliberately non-binding

An estimate is what you send when the work is real but the scope isn't locked yet — early conversations, discovery calls, or "just want a sense of cost" questions before a project is fully defined. Where a quote is a number you'll honor, an estimate is a range you're explicitly allowed to move away from once you know more: "typically $6,000–$9,000 depending on page count and integrations." The range itself is doing work — it filters out prospects whose budget is nowhere close, without forcing you to commit to a figure before you've scoped anything. The mistake to avoid is treating an estimate as a quote in disguise: if you give a tight range and then the real number comes in well outside it, you'll spend the first week of the relationship rebuilding trust instead of doing the work. Keep the range wide enough to be honest, and always pair it with a next step — a discovery call or a scoping session — that turns the estimate into either a quote or a proposal.

## Proposal: the full sell document

A proposal is what you send for anything substantial enough to need a case, not just a number — multi-week projects, retainers, or any engagement over roughly $3,000–$5,000, where the client is choosing between you and at least one alternative (another freelancer, an agency, or doing it in-house). A proposal earns its length: the problem as you understand it, your approach, a scoped deliverable list, timeline, pricing (often presented in tiers — see [Step 6 of pricing a project](/blog/how-to-price-a-freelance-project) if you haven't set those up yet), and terms. It's a selling document as much as a pricing one, which is exactly why it's the wrong tool for a fast qualifying question — nobody wants to read five pages to find out if you're in their budget. ## A simple rule for which one to send

If you can answer "how much" in one line without more information, send a quote. If you genuinely don't know the number yet because the scope isn't defined, send an estimate — and use it to get to a scoping conversation. If the engagement is big enough that you need to make a case for why it's you and why it's worth the number, send a proposal. Getting this sequencing right also protects your rate: a quote sent too early on an undefined project locks you into a number before you understand the real work, and a full proposal sent to a prospect who just wanted a ballpark reads as overkill before they've decided you're worth the read.
