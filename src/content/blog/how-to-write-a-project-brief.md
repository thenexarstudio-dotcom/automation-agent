## The brief's actual job

A project brief isn't a scope of work, and it isn't a proposal — it comes before both. Its job is to get everyone (you, the client, and anyone else with sign-off) aligned on what's being built and why, before you spend time scoping deliverables or pricing anything. A brief that does its job gets approved in one pass. A brief that doesn't gets sent back with "can we also..." comments, which is usually a sign that something essential was missing, not that the client is being difficult. If you're finding yourself in a second or third round of revisions before a client will sign off, the fix is almost always structural: a specific section was skipped, not that the writing needs to be more persuasive.

## Section 1: Objective, not activity

Open with what success looks like, not what you'll be doing. "Redesign the checkout flow" is an activity. "Reduce checkout abandonment enough to recover an estimated $40K in annual revenue" is an objective. Activities are easy to agree to and hard to hold anyone accountable to; objectives give both sides a shared reason the project exists, and they're what you'll refer back to later if the client starts requesting things that don't serve the actual goal. If the client can't articulate the objective clearly during discovery, that's worth surfacing before you write the brief — a project without a stated objective is a project that's hard to finish, because there's no way to agree you're done.

## Section 2: Audience and context

Who is this actually for, and what do they need to already know or believe? A brief for an internal dashboard used by three analysts is a completely different project than one for a public-facing tool used by thousands of first-time visitors, even if the deliverable list looks similar on paper. This section also captures competitive or market context — what exists already, what the client likes or dislikes about it, and what "better" means to them specifically, since that word means something different to every client.

## Section 3: Deliverables, stated plainly

A short, plain-language list of what will exist when the project is done — not the itemized, contractual version that belongs in a scope of work, but enough specificity that the client can picture the actual output. "A 12-page marketing site with a blog" is a brief-level deliverable; the page-by-page breakdown with revision counts and exclusions comes later, once the brief is approved and you're writing the [Scope of Work](/blog/how-to-write-a-scope-of-work). Keeping the brief at this level of detail is what makes it fast to approve — it's specific enough to be real, but not so granular that it invites line-by-line negotiation before the project has even been greenlit.

## Section 4: Constraints

Budget range, timeline, required tools or platforms, brand or legal guardrails, and anyone whose approval is required before launch. This section prevents the single most common cause of late-stage rework: discovering in week four that legal needs to review everything, or that the client's platform can't support a feature you scoped around. Ask for this explicitly during discovery rather than assuming it'll come up — clients routinely forget to mention a constraint until it's violated. ## Section 5: Success criteria

How will you and the client both know the project worked — not just "delivered," but "worked"? Tie this back to the objective in Section 1: if the objective was reducing checkout abandonment, the success criteria might be a specific percentage drop measured 30 days post-launch. Not every project has a clean metric, and that's fine — but even a qualitative criterion ("client's team can update the site without developer help") gives you something concrete to point to at the end of the engagement, rather than leaving "done" open to interpretation.

## Keep it to one page

A brief that runs past a page is usually smuggling in scope-of-work detail it doesn't need yet, and it also gets read less carefully — five sections, plain language, one page a client can approve on a single read. Once it's signed off, it becomes the reference point for the scope of work and the proposal that follow, so anything that drifts from it later is easy to spot and name as a change rather than an argument.
