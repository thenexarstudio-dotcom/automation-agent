## The kickoff sets the operating rules

Most project problems — missed deadlines, scope creep, the client who goes silent for three weeks — trace back to a kickoff that never happened or happened badly. The kickoff is where you establish how the project will actually run: who decides, how you'll communicate, and what "done" looks like. Skip it and you'll relitigate those questions one painful email at a time. Run it well and the rest of the project has a rulebook to fall back on.

## Prepare before anyone joins the call

A kickoff is not the place to *gather* basic information — it's where you *confirm* it. Send an [onboarding questionnaire](/templates/client-onboarding-questionnaire) in advance so you arrive already knowing the client's goals, constraints, assets, and stakeholders. Walking in cold and asking "so, what are we hoping to achieve?" signals that the client is about to do your discovery for you. Walking in with a draft understanding to validate signals control.

## Run the agenda in five blocks

Keep the meeting to an hour, and keep it structured. Five blocks cover everything that matters:

**1. Goals and definition of success.** State the outcome the project is meant to produce and get explicit agreement. If the client can't name what success looks like, that's the most important thing you'll uncover all day — surface it now, not at delivery.

**2. Scope and boundaries.** Walk the deliverables, then walk the exclusions just as deliberately. Naming what you're *not* doing prevents more disputes than anything else you'll say. Reference the signed scope so everyone's looking at the same list.

**3. Roles and decision-makers.** Establish who has sign-off authority and who the single point of contact is. Projects stall when feedback arrives from five people who disagree with each other — agree now that approvals route through one person.

**4. Process and cadence.** Set the communication rhythm: how often you'll update them, on what channel, and what your response times are. Committing to a weekly update pre-empts the "any progress?" check-ins that interrupt real work.

**5. Timeline and dependencies.** Confirm key dates and, critically, what you need *from the client* to hit them. Late client input is a top cause of blown deadlines — name every dependency and attach a date to it while everyone's paying attention.

## Make the change process explicit — out loud

Somewhere in the scope block, say plainly what happens when a request falls outside the agreed work: it gets a written request, a priced change order, and approval before anyone starts. Saying this warmly during the kickoff — when there's no dispute and no tension — makes it feel like process rather than confrontation later. ## Close with confirmed next steps

End every kickoff with three things named out loud: the immediate next action, who owns it, and by when. Then send a short written recap the same day — goals, decisions, owners, dates. That recap becomes the reference point everyone checks against, and it's your first proof that this project is going to be run, not just worked.
