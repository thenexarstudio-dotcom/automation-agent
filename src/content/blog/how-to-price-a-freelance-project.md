## The problem with guessing

Ask most freelancers how they arrived at their rate, and the honest answer is some combination of "what felt reasonable," "what a friend charges," or "what I saw on a forum." None of those account for what you actually need to earn or how much time you actually have available to bill — which is why so many freelancers stay busy without being profitable.

## Step 1: Start from your income target, not your rate

Decide what you actually want to earn this year, after expenses. Not what sounds impressive — what you need and want, based on last year's actuals if you have them.

## Step 2: Get honest about billable capacity

A calendar year has roughly 260 working days. Subtract time for sales and marketing, admin, professional development, sick days, and vacation, and most solo freelancers land at 100–140 realistically billable days — far fewer than it feels like when you're planning a year in the abstract.

## Step 3: Add real overhead

Software subscriptions, insurance, self-employment tax burden, equipment — these come out of revenue before it becomes take-home income, and are commonly under-budgeted.

## Step 4: Calculate the day rate

Day rate = (income target + overhead) / billable days. This turns three abstract numbers into one concrete rate you can defend — try the free [Day Rate Calculator](/tools/freelance-day-rate-calculator) to run your own numbers.

## Step 5: Price projects, not hours

Once you have a day rate, estimate the project in days, multiply by the day rate, and add a margin (10–25%) for risk and packaging. Present this as a single project investment — not hours times a rate — because an hourly framing invites the client to negotiate your time instead of evaluating your outcome. The [Consulting Proposal Template](/templates/consulting-proposal-template) shows exactly where this number goes and how to frame it.

## Step 6: Offer tiers, not one number

A single price is a yes/no decision. Three tiers (starter/standard/premium) turn it into a choice — and consistently produce a higher average sale, because the middle tier reads as the reasonable option. See the [Rate Card Template](/templates/rate-card-template) for the full structure.
