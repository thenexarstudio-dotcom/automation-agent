## The real risk in a single end-of-project invoice

When the entire fee is due only at delivery, you've effectively extended the client interest-free credit for the length of the project — weeks or months of unpaid work sitting on your books, with your only recourse being to invoice after everything is already done. If the client stalls, disappears, or disputes scope right at the finish line, the exposure is the whole project, not a slice of it. Milestone payments exist to shrink that exposure to something you could actually absorb if one invoice went sideways.

## Start with a deposit before any work begins

A deposit — commonly 25–50% of the fixed fee — collected before kickoff isn't just cash flow, it's a signal. A client willing to pay upfront is a client committed to the engagement; one who pushes back on a standard deposit is telling you something about how the rest of the project is likely to go. State the deposit requirement in your [Freelance Service Agreement Template](/templates/freelance-contract-template) so it's a standing term, not a negotiation you have to reopen on every new project.

## Break the fee into milestones, not time phases

A common mistake is splitting a fee by calendar time — "half now, half in a month" — instead of by deliverable. Time-based splits still leave you exposed if the project runs long, and they don't create a natural checkpoint for the client to review and sign off. Instead, tie each payment to something concrete and shippable: a completed discovery phase, an approved first draft, a delivered final asset. Each milestone should map directly to a line in your [Scope of Work Template](/templates/scope-of-work-template), so both sides can point to the same document and agree a milestone is actually done — not argue about it.

## Gate the next milestone behind payment for the last one

This is the part freelancers skip out of politeness, and it's the part that actually protects you: don't start work on milestone two until milestone one is paid — not "invoiced," paid. A standard line in your kickoff communication ("work on the next phase begins once the milestone invoice clears") sets the expectation before it's ever tested, so enforcing it later doesn't feel like an accusation. Clients who work with vendors regularly expect this; treating it as routine is what keeps it from reading as adversarial.

## How many milestones is right

Two milestones — deposit plus final — is the minimum, but it leaves the single largest chunk of risk sitting at the very end. Three to five milestones, spaced around genuinely distinct deliverables, is usually the sweet spot: enough to keep any one unpaid balance small, without turning the project into an invoicing chore. For anything running longer than six to eight weeks, or priced above what you'd comfortably front for a month unpaid, lean toward more milestones rather than fewer.

## Price scope drift into the schedule, not into a milestone

Milestone payments only protect the scope you already priced. If a client adds a meaningful request mid-milestone, that's scope creep, not a small favor — run the [Scope Creep Cost Calculator](/tools/scope-creep-cost-calculator) to see what unbilled additions actually cost, and route anything real through a [Change Order Template](/templates/change-order-template) before it quietly expands a milestone you already agreed a price for.

## Put the schedule in writing, twice

The milestone schedule needs to live in two places with matching language: the contract, so it's enforceable, and the invoice you send at each trigger, so there's no ambiguity about what's being billed and why. A [Milestone Payment Schedule Template](/templates/milestone-payment-schedule-template) turns this into something you build once during scoping and reuse on every fixed-fee project — instead of improvising a new split, and a new set of assumptions, each time.
