## Start by checking what you actually agreed to

Before writing a single email, reread your signed agreement. Confirm the payment terms, the due date that applies, whether a late fee was specified, and whether the contract gives you the right to pause work over nonpayment. This isn't a formality — it's what turns your next email from a request into an enforceable reminder that cites specific, agreed-upon terms, which lands very differently on the other end. If your [Freelance Contract Template](/templates/freelance-contract-template) doesn't currently spell these out, fix that for the next engagement before you deal with this one.

## Days 1–7 past due: a neutral reminder

The first message should assume good faith — a missed invoice, a change in accounts payable contact, an email that landed in a spam filter. Keep it short, attach the original invoice, and ask a direct question: has this been received, and is there anything blocking payment? No urgency in the tone yet. Most late payments resolve at this stage for reasons that have nothing to do with the client avoiding you, so there's no need to escalate the tone before you've confirmed there's actually a problem.

## Day 14: a firmer written notice

If the first reminder goes unanswered, the second message changes in two ways: it references the specific payment terms in your signed agreement, and it states a concrete consequence if payment doesn't arrive by a stated date — commonly a late fee taking effect or work pausing. This isn't about sounding angry; it's about being specific and unambiguous, in writing, so there's a clear record of what was communicated and when. Having a set of [Late Payment Reminder Emails](/templates/late-payment-reminder-emails) ready in advance means you're not drafting this message from scratch while frustrated, which tends to produce worse emails than a calm template you wrote in advance.

## Stop work — and say so in writing

If a client is materially overdue and unresponsive, pausing work is usually the single most effective lever you have, and it's one most freelancers are reluctant to pull. Continuing to deliver while unpaid removes any incentive the client has to resolve the invoice — from their side, the arrangement is working fine. State clearly, in writing, that work is paused pending payment and exactly what needs to happen to resume it. This should already be a right your contract gives you; if it isn't, add it before your next engagement.

## Offer a structured path before you escalate further

Before moving to formal collection, it's often worth offering a partial payment plan, especially for a client who's genuinely short on cash rather than avoiding you outright. A partial payment now plus a written schedule for the rest is a better outcome than an all-or-nothing standoff, and it preserves the option of future work if the relationship is otherwise sound. Put the schedule in writing and get it acknowledged in reply — a verbal agreement here is worth very little.

## When to involve collections or small claims

If the amount owed is significant and every earlier step has failed, a demand letter from an attorney or a small claims filing is the next step — small claims limits vary by jurisdiction but commonly cover freelance invoice amounts, and the process is designed to be usable without a lawyer. A signed contract with clear terms and a paper trail of dated reminders is exactly what makes a claim like this straightforward to win, which is the whole argument for having both in place well before you ever need them.

## Prevent the repeat

Once this invoice is resolved, look at why it happened. Late payment is frequently a process problem more than a character problem — the fix usually lives in the invoice itself, the intake process, and the terms both parties signed. If prevention is the bigger priority, keep every future invoice on the [Invoice Template](/templates/invoice-template) with terms that match your contract exactly, and treat this escalation ladder as the plan you hope never to need again.
