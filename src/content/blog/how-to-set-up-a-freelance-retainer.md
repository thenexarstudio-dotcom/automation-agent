## Why project work keeps you on a treadmill

Every project has an end date, which means every project has a cliff. Finish the work, send the final invoice, and you're back to prospecting from zero — regardless of how good the result was. That feast-or-famine cycle isn't a sign you're bad at sales; it's a structural feature of selling one-off engagements. A retainer removes the cliff by turning a client relationship into a recurring line item instead of a series of restarts.

## What a retainer actually sells

A retainer is not "a discount for buying in bulk." You're selling one of two things, and it matters which:

**Access.** The client pays for guaranteed availability — a set number of hours or days per month, first call on your calendar, a promised response time. This suits advisory, maintenance, and on-call work.

**Outcomes.** The client pays for a recurring deliverable — four blog posts, a monthly reporting cycle, an ongoing optimization loop. This suits productized, repeatable work.

Decide which one you're selling before you name a price, because it changes what you're on the hook for. Access retainers cap your *time*; outcome retainers cap your *scope*. Blur the two and you'll end up delivering unlimited outcomes on a fixed-hours budget.

## Scoping monthly capacity honestly

The most common retainer mistake is selling more hours than you can consistently deliver. Take your realistic billable capacity for the month and commit only a fraction of it to any single retainer — leaving room for project work, overruns, and the client who needs you unexpectedly. If your day rate math is fuzzy, run it first with the [Day Rate Calculator](/tools/freelance-day-rate-calculator) so the monthly number rests on real capacity, not a hopeful guess.

State the included capacity as a specific number (hours or days), and state plainly what happens when it's exceeded: work pauses until next cycle, or rolls to an hourly overage rate. Undefined overflow is where retainers quietly become unpaid work.

## Handle rollover and the pause clause

Two clauses prevent most retainer disputes. First, **rollover**: do unused hours carry to next month or expire? Expiring hours are cleaner for you and standard in the industry — say so up front. Second, **the pause and cancellation terms**: a 30-day written notice period protects your income from a client who wakes up one morning and decides to stop. Both belong in writing, not in a friendly verbal understanding. ## Price it above the pro-rated project rate

Counterintuitively, a retainer should often cost *more* per hour than the same work billed by project, not less. The client is buying certainty — reserved capacity and a standing priority claim on your time — and certainty is worth a premium. Discounting a retainer trains your best clients to value your availability at a bargain rate, which is the opposite of what recurring revenue is for.

## Pitch it at the right moment

The easiest retainer to sell is the one you propose right as a successful project wraps. The client has proof you deliver, momentum is high, and the natural question — "what happens next?" — is already on the table.
