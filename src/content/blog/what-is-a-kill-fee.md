## What a kill fee actually is

A kill fee is a pre-agreed payment a client owes if they cancel or terminate a project partway through — compensation for the work already done, the calendar time you blocked off, and the other clients you turned away to take the project. It's distinct from a deposit, which is paid upfront before work starts, and from a normal final invoice, which pays for completed deliverables. A kill fee specifically covers the gap between what's been delivered and what you're actually owed when a project ends early.

## When you actually need one

Kill fees matter most on projects where you've reserved meaningful calendar time in advance — retainers, blocked-out engagements, anything where saying yes to this client meant saying no to other work you could have taken instead. They matter less on small, fast projects billed strictly [milestone by milestone](/blog/milestone-payments-for-freelancers), where the payment structure already limits your exposure to whatever's currently in progress. Even then, a kill fee is worth having as a backstop for wherever the current milestone leaves off, especially on longer or exclusive engagements where the calendar cost of cancellation is real.

## Common kill fee structures

Three structures cover most freelance and consulting agreements:

**Flat percentage of remaining fee.** A stated percentage — commonly 25–50% — of whatever's left unbilled on the contract, regardless of when the cancellation happens. Simple to state and easy for a client to understand upfront.

**Sliding scale by project stage.** A lower percentage if canceled early, before significant work has started, rising the further into the project it happens — reflecting that late cancellation costs you more in lost opportunity and sunk time. A common set of bands is 25% in the first third, 50% in the middle third, and 75% or more in the final third.

**Notice-period buyout.** Instead of a percentage of the whole fee, the client owes for a stated notice period — commonly two to four weeks — at your standard rate, on top of anything already delivered. This is the more common structure on retainers, where "remaining fee" isn't a fixed, known number to take a percentage of.

## How much should yours be

There's no single right number, but the fee should cover two things: work already done that isn't otherwise billable, and the opportunity cost of calendar time you blocked off and can't easily refill on short notice. As a starting point, 25–50% of the remaining contract value is standard for project work; retainers more often use a notice-period buyout instead. If you're unsure what your time is actually worth for this calculation, run the [Day Rate Calculator](/tools/freelance-day-rate-calculator) — a kill fee stated in relation to your real day rate is easier to defend to a client than one that was pulled from a rough feeling.

## Where to put it, and how to state it

State the kill fee as a specific number or percentage, tied to a specific trigger — "if the client terminates this agreement before completion" — not vague language like "a reasonable fee," which invites a dispute at exactly the moment you need clarity most. It belongs in the termination clause of your [Freelance Service Agreement Template](/templates/freelance-contract-template), referenced against the same milestone or phase language used in your [Scope of Work Template](/templates/scope-of-work-template), so there's no ambiguity about what "remaining" means when the moment actually arrives.

## A note on legal advice

This is general guidance on how freelancers and consultants commonly structure kill fees — not legal advice. Enforceability of a kill fee clause can depend on your jurisdiction and the specifics of your agreement. If a client relationship is high-value enough that the difference matters, it's worth having a lawyer review the termination language in your contract before you need it, rather than after a cancellation is already underway.
