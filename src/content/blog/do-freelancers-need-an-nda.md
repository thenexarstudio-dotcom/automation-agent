## What an NDA actually does — and doesn't

A nondisclosure agreement obligates you to keep specific information confidential and not use it outside the relationship it was shared for. That's it. It doesn't cover ownership of your deliverables, payment terms, liability, or what happens if the project ends early — those belong in a service agreement, not an NDA. Freelancers sometimes treat a signed NDA as if it were the whole contract, sign it, and only later realize nothing governs price, scope, or payment. The two documents do different jobs, and a client asking for one isn't asking you to skip the other.

## When you genuinely need one

An NDA earns its place when a client is sharing something that would cause real harm if it leaked before it's public: unreleased product details, financial figures, an unannounced acquisition, source code, or customer data. If a discovery call surfaces information like this before any contract exists, that's the moment to have a signed NDA in place — not the general pitch deck a prospect hands you, but specifics that would matter if a competitor saw them. In those cases, sign it and move on; it costs you nothing beyond a few minutes and formalizes an obligation you'd honor anyway.

## When it's overkill — and slows the deal down

Plenty of NDA requests aren't really about protecting sensitive information — they're a default step in a company's procurement process, sent to every vendor before a single call happens, covering nothing more sensitive than the fact that a conversation took place. Signing one of these costs you little, but if a prospect insists on a heavily lawyered, one-sided NDA before you've had a single qualifying conversation, that's worth noticing: it's often a preview of how the rest of the engagement will be negotiated. A short discovery call rarely needs a five-page mutual NDA with indemnification clauses attached.

## Red flags to check before you sign

Read every NDA a client sends you before signing, even a "standard" one — templates get repurposed carelessly and unfavorable clauses survive multiple client relationships without anyone noticing. Watch for a few specific problems: no expiration date (confidentiality that never ends is unusual and usually unnecessary), a definition of "confidential information" broad enough to include your own pre-existing methods and general knowledge, and a non-compete or non-solicit clause quietly inserted into what's supposed to be a confidentiality agreement. None of these are unheard of — but each one is a fair thing to ask to strike or narrow before you sign.

## Mutual vs. one-way — ask for mutual

Most NDAs a client sends are one-way: you're bound to confidentiality, they're not. That's worth questioning. If you'll be sharing your own process, pricing, or proprietary frameworks with them during the project, a mutual NDA protects you too, and asking for one is a completely normal, low-friction request — most clients will simply agree.
