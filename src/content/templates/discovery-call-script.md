## Why most discovery calls waste both people's time

A discovery call has exactly one job: find out, in 20-30 minutes, whether this prospect and this problem are a genuine fit for what you do, at a budget and timeline that make sense. Most calls fail that job in one of two ways — the freelancer either talks too much and turns it into a pitch, or keeps things so friendly that nobody asks the specific questions that would actually reveal a mismatch. Either way, you end up writing a proposal for someone who was never going to buy, or worse, winning a project that was underpriced or underscoped from the first conversation.

## What the call needs to establish

### The real problem, not the presenting one
Prospects usually open with a symptom ("we need a new website") rather than the underlying business problem ("our current site isn't converting and we don't know why"). Your first round of questions should dig past the request to the outcome they actually need — it's the difference between quoting a deliverable and quoting a result.

### Budget reality
You don't need an exact number, but you need enough signal to know whether their expectations and your rates are in the same universe. Knowing your own numbers cold — from the [freelance day rate calculator](/tools/freelance-day-rate-calculator) — makes it much easier to steer this part of the conversation without guessing.

### Decision-making authority
Find out early whether you're talking to the person who signs off, or to someone who will relay your proposal to a decision-maker you've never spoken to. This changes how you close the call and who you loop in next.

### Timeline and urgency
A prospect with no real deadline behaves very differently in a sales process than one with a fixed date driving the decision. Ask directly what happens if this doesn't get solved by a specific point.

## A four-part structure that works

1. **Open** — one line on why you're both here, then hand the floor to them.
2. **Diagnose** — open questions on the problem, its cost, and what's been tried.
3. **Qualify** — budget signal, decision process, and timeline, asked plainly.
4. **Close** — name the clear next step (proposal, no fit, or a second call) before you hang up.

## What happens after the call

If it's a fit, the next step is a [consulting proposal](/templates/consulting-proposal-template) built directly from what you heard, followed by the [client onboarding questionnaire](/templates/client-onboarding-questionnaire) to gather the detail a call can't cover, and eventually a [scope of work](/templates/scope-of-work-template) that turns the conversation into deliverables. If it's not a fit, say so on the call rather than in a delayed email — a clear "this isn't a match" is more respectful of everyone's time than a proposal neither side expects to move forward.

## Where this fits in your document stack

The discovery call is upstream of everything else in the Kit — it's the filter that decides whether a prospect is worth a proposal at all. Getting the questions right here saves the hours you'd otherwise spend writing detailed scopes and pricing for engagements that were never going to close.
