## Why most proposals lose the deal before the client reaches the price

A proposal isn't a summary of the sales call — it's the document that removes the client's last reasons to say "let me think about it." Most freelance proposals fail for one of three reasons: they restate generic capabilities instead of the client's specific problem, they bury the price on page four, or they leave the next step vague. This template fixes all three.

## The structure

### 1. Situation summary
Two or three sentences, in the client's language, describing the problem as they described it to you. Not your services — their situation. This is what makes a client feel like the document was written for them, not copy-pasted.

> "[Client] is preparing to launch [initiative] by [date], but the team currently lacks [specific gap] — which puts [specific business risk] at risk if it isn't resolved before launch."

### 2. Proposed approach
Three to five bullet points describing *how* you'll close that gap — phases or workstreams, not task lists. Specificity here is what differentiates you from a generic freelancer; vague bullets ("strategy," "execution," "reporting") read as interchangeable with anyone else's proposal.

### 3. Deliverables
A bulleted list of concrete, nameable artifacts the client will have in hand at the end — documents, systems, dashboards, decisions made. If you can't name the artifact, the deliverable is too vague to bill against later.

### 4. Timeline
A simple table: phase, deliverable, target date. Round to weeks, not days, unless the engagement is under two weeks — exact dates you'll inevitably slip create more friction than ranges.

### 5. Investment
State the fee as a total project investment, not an hourly rate, whenever the engagement is scoped enough to price that way (hourly pricing invites clients to negotiate your time instead of your outcome). Include payment terms directly beneath it — e.g., "50% to begin, 50% at delivery" — so there's no separate negotiation later.

### 6. What's not included
One short paragraph naming what's explicitly out of scope. This single section prevents more scope creep than any clause in the contract that follows — clients rarely push on things a document has already told them aren't included.

### 7. Next step
One sentence with a single, unambiguous call to action and a deadline: "To begin on [date], I'll need a signed agreement and the first invoice paid by [date]." Never end a proposal with "let me know if you have questions" — that's an invitation to go quiet.

## What to do after they say yes

The proposal's job ends the moment the client agrees to move forward. At that point, two documents take over: the **Service Agreement** makes the relationship contractually real, and the **Scope of Work** operationalizes exactly what was promised above so there's a single shared reference point if the engagement ever drifts.
