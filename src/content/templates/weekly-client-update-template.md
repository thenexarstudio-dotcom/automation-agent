## Why the weekly update is the highest-ROI email you send

Client anxiety doesn't come from bad news — it comes from silence. A client who hasn't heard from you in ten days starts to wonder, and that wondering is what produces the "just checking in — where are we?" email, the loss of trust, and eventually the micromanagement. A short, predictable weekly update pre-empts all of it for about five minutes of effort. It's the cheapest relationship insurance in freelancing, and almost nobody sends it consistently.

## What a good update does beyond reassurance

### It creates a timeline record
Every update timestamps what was done, what's next, and — crucially — what you're waiting on from the client. When a deadline slips because client feedback arrived a week late, the written trail shows exactly where the delay originated. This is the same protection the [scope of work](/templates/scope-of-work-template) gives you, extended week by week: a dependency named in Friday's update is not deniable later.

### It surfaces scope drift early
A weekly "here's what's in progress" naturally exposes requests that have quietly crept beyond the agreed scope, while they're still small enough to handle cleanly. Catching drift in week two means a calm [change order](/templates/change-order-template); catching it at final delivery means an argument. The update is your early-warning system.

### It keeps decisions moving
A standing "Need from you" line, with a date attached, turns vague waiting into a specific ask the client can act on. Most feedback delays aren't refusals — they're the client not realizing they're the blocker. Naming it, in writing, every week, is what keeps a project from stalling on a decision nobody knew was theirs to make.

## The structure that keeps it to five minutes

The template is deliberately short and always the same five sections: what got done this week, what's next, any blockers or risks, what you need from the client (with dates), and — on fixed-price or retainer work — a one-line status on scope or hours. Sameness is the point: a fixed structure means you're filling in a form, not composing an email, so it actually happens every week instead of only when there's news.
