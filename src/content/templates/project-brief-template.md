## Why scoping without a brief produces the wrong deliverables

Most scope disputes don't start with a vague SOW — they start earlier, with a project nobody actually defined. A freelancer who scopes work straight from a sales call is scoping their own interpretation of what the client said, not what the client meant. The project brief closes that gap: a short, client-facing document that forces goals, audience, and constraints onto paper *before* a single deliverable gets written down, so the thing you eventually scope is the thing the client actually needs.

## What belongs in a project brief

### The goal, in the client's words
One or two sentences on what this project is meant to achieve — not the deliverable, the outcome behind it. "A new website" is a deliverable; "convert more visitors booking discovery calls" is the goal a website might serve. Anchoring on the goal early keeps every later scoping decision traceable back to something the client actually cares about.

### Audience
Who the end result is actually for — customers, internal stakeholders, a specific buyer segment. A deliverable built for the wrong audience is technically complete and still a failure; naming the audience up front prevents that specific, expensive mistake.

### Background and context
A short paragraph on what's been tried before, what currently exists, and why this project is happening now. This is where you catch the "we tried this two years ago and it didn't work" detail that would otherwise surface midway through the engagement.

### Constraints
Budget range, timeline, brand or technical requirements, anything non-negotiable. Constraints named in the brief become inputs to your scope and price; constraints discovered mid-project become scope creep and delay.

### Success criteria
The specific, ideally measurable definition of "done and working" — a metric, a stakeholder sign-off, a launch date. This is the single most valuable section: it's what you'll hold the finished work against, and what later becomes the backbone of your [project completion report](/templates/project-completion-report).

### Out of scope (as far as the client knows now)
A short list of adjacent things the client does *not* expect from this project. Clients rarely think to volunteer this, but asking directly here surfaces assumptions before they turn into disputes later.

## How the brief feeds the rest of the engagement

The brief isn't a standalone artifact — it's the raw material for everything that follows. Its goals and constraints turn directly into the deliverables and exclusions of your [scope of work](/templates/scope-of-work-template) (the [SOW Generator](/tools/sow-generator) can turn a filled-out brief into a first-draft SOW in minutes), and its success criteria become the yardstick your final [project completion report](/templates/project-completion-report) reports against. Skipping it doesn't save time — it just moves the goal-clarifying conversation to the middle of the project, where it's far more expensive to have.

## When to send it

Send the brief before you price the work, ideally before or alongside your first discovery call. A client who's already written down their goal, audience, and constraints turns a vague "what are you looking for" conversation into a focused one about approach and cost — and gives you a paper trail for what "success" meant if the definition ever gets renegotiated later.
