## Why scope creep is a documentation problem, not a client problem

Scope creep rarely comes from bad clients — it comes from under-specified agreements. When "deliverables" is a vague paragraph instead of an itemized list, every request feels reasonable to the client, because nothing on paper says otherwise. A tight SOW doesn't make you rigid; it gives both sides a shared, specific reference point to check requests against.

## What belongs in a scope of work

### Deliverables (itemized, not summarized)
List every deliverable as its own line, each phrased so completion is objectively checkable:
- Format (document, deck, dashboard, code, etc.)
- Length or scale where relevant (page count, number of variants, number of revisions)
- The specific outcome it represents

### Explicitly out of scope
A short list naming adjacent work you are *not* doing. This is the section that prevents the most disputes — clients rarely argue that something is in scope when the document explicitly says it isn't.

### Revision policy
State a specific number of revision rounds per deliverable (commonly two) and what happens beyond that — typically billed at your standard rate or via a change order. Unlimited revisions is the single most common way freelancers turn a fixed-price project into an unpaid hourly one.

### Timeline and dependencies
List key dates *and* what you need from the client to hit them (assets, approvals, access). Late client input is one of the most common — and most avoidable — causes of blown deadlines; naming the dependency up front puts responsibility for the delay where it belongs.

### Assumptions
Any conditions your price and timeline depend on (e.g., "assumes one primary point of contact," "assumes existing brand assets are final"). If an assumption turns out false, that's your opening to re-scope rather than absorb the difference.

### Change process
One paragraph describing how new requests get handled: written request → you send a Change Order with added cost/time → client approves before work begins. Reference the Change Order Template directly here so there's no ambiguity about the mechanism.

## How this fits with the rest of the Kit

The SOW is referenced *inside* the Service Agreement (as "Exhibit A" or similar) so it carries contractual weight, and any request outside it routes through the Change Order Template rather than a scope conversation happening in a Slack thread that nobody can point back to later.
